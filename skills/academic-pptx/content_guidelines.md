# Academic Deck Content Guide

This guide is part of the Rylai Codex-Hermes-Claude academic presentation workflow.
Use it to decide what belongs in the talk before styling slides.

## 1. Establish the Central Position

Write three sentences:

1. The problem the audience should care about.
2. The strongest conclusion supported by the work.
3. The boundary beyond which the conclusion should not be extended.

These sentences define the talk's center. A main-deck slide should clarify the
problem, establish the conclusion, show its support, explain its limits, or
translate it into an implication.

## 2. Keep a Claim Ledger

Track each important claim with:

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | Exact statement the slide will make |
| Evidence | Figure, table, method, quotation, or source |
| Status | Established, preliminary, proposed, or unknown |
| Scope | Population, conditions, dataset, or assumptions |
| Citation | Source details sufficient for the deck |
| Risk | Likely objection or possible misinterpretation |

Remove unsupported claims. If a claim is useful but preliminary, label it
honestly rather than presenting it as settled.

## 3. Convert Documents into a Talk

A paper and a presentation serve different reading conditions. Do not preserve
the manuscript section order automatically.

For each candidate item, ask:

- Does the audience need this to understand the central result?
- Is it better spoken, shown, placed in notes, or moved to the appendix?
- What comparison makes the evidence interpretable?
- What term or assumption must be introduced first?
- What would be lost if this item were removed?

Compress literature review into the minimum context needed to define the gap.
Expand the interpretation of decisive results, because that is where a live
audience cannot pause and reread.

## 4. Write Message Titles

A content-slide title should tell the audience what to understand from the
slide. Use a neutral topic title only when the slide genuinely introduces a
section or asks an open question.

Good title functions include:

- state a measured result;
- identify a limitation;
- define a research question;
- explain why a method choice matters;
- connect evidence to an implication.

Avoid exaggerated certainty, promotional adjectives, and novelty claims that
the available sources do not establish.

## 5. Protect Evidence Integrity

- Preserve units, denominators, sample sizes, and uncertainty.
- State whether values are measured, estimated, simulated, or illustrative.
- Keep baseline and treatment definitions visible.
- Do not redraw a chart in a way that changes the apparent comparison.
- Avoid truncated axes unless the reason is clear and the scale is explicit.
- Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance.
- Use the source's terminology unless a change is defined.
- Mark unavailable or redacted data rather than substituting invented values.

When calculations are needed, retain the formula and source values in the build
artifact or notes so the result can be reproduced.

## 6. Handle Citations

Place a compact citation on the slide that uses the material. Include enough
information for the audience to identify the source, then provide full
references in the final slides or accompanying document.

Cite:

- external figures, images, tables, and datasets;
- quotations and paraphrased claims;
- methods or scales associated with another work;
- factual context that is not common knowledge.

For the presenter's own unpublished work, label its status and date. Never imply
peer review or publication that has not occurred.

## 7. Control Information Density

Use one dominant reading path per slide. A slide may contain multiple objects
when they are part of one comparison, but it should not ask the audience to
solve several unrelated tasks.

Reduce density in this order:

1. remove repeated prose;
2. move detail to speaker notes;
3. split distinct reasoning steps;
4. move secondary evidence to the appendix;
5. simplify labels while preserving meaning.

Do not solve density by shrinking text until it is technically inside the box.

## 8. Present Methods Clearly

Methods slides should answer:

- what enters the process;
- what is changed, measured, or inferred;
- which choice is novel or consequential;
- what leaves the process;
- how validity is assessed.

Use a process diagram for sequence, an architecture diagram for relationships,
an equation for formal dependency, and a table for comparable configuration.
Choose the form that matches the reasoning.

## 9. Present Results as Decisions

Every result slide needs:

- a stated finding;
- the comparison that supports it;
- visible evidence;
- uncertainty or qualification;
- one sentence of interpretation.

If a chart contains several series, emphasize only the series needed for the
current message and mute the rest without hiding relevant counterevidence.

## 10. Treat Limitations Precisely

Separate:

- known limits demonstrated by the work;
- conditions that were not tested;
- risks introduced by measurement or design;
- future work that would reduce uncertainty.

Do not use a limitations slide as ritual self-criticism. Connect each limitation
to how the audience should interpret the conclusion.

## 11. Plan Speaker Notes

Notes can contain:

- the opening sentence for the slide;
- explanation of abbreviations;
- transitions;
- details omitted from the visual;
- caveats and likely questions;
- a target time.

Notes should support delivery, not duplicate every visible word.

## 12. Time the Argument

Allocate time according to importance and cognitive load. As a starting point:

- orient the audience and define the question;
- explain only the method needed to trust the evidence;
- spend the largest share on results and interpretation;
- reserve a clear close and transition to questions.

Rehearse aloud. If the talk is too long, remove material from the main deck.
Speaking faster usually reduces comprehension and weakens the argument.

## Content Review Checklist

- The central position and its boundary are explicit.
- Each major claim has traceable evidence.
- Message titles form a coherent sequence.
- Terms appear only after they are introduced.
- Methods provide enough information to judge validity.
- Results show comparisons, uncertainty, and interpretation.
- Citations sit beside the content they support.
- Limitations change how the conclusion is understood.
- The final slide states the answer, evidence, and implication.
- Appendix material supports questions without interrupting the main talk.
